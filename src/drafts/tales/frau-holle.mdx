<Initial>

When it snows in Germany, people sometimes say that Frau Holle is shaking out her bed.
This is her story: two sisters, one who works gladly and one who doesn't, and what each of
them brings home. Listen for <span lang="de">*gern*</span> and <span lang="de">*nicht
gern*</span>, and for the bed from lesson 5.

</Initial>

## Down the well

A widow had two daughters. One was her own, and lazy. The other was her stepdaughter,
and she did all the work in the house. Every day she sat by the well and spun until her
fingers bled. One day the spindle slipped from her bloody hand and fell into the well.
Her stepmother told her to fetch it back, so she jumped in after it.

She woke in a beautiful meadow full of sunshine. She walked on, and came to an oven full of
bread. The bread was calling out.

<Dialogue title="The oven" setting="A sunny meadow at the bottom of the well">
  <Line who="The bread" de="Hallo! Hallo! Wir sind warm! Wir sind lecker!" en="Hello! Hello! We're warm! We're delicious!" />
</Dialogue>

The loaves were done, and about to burn. So she took them all out with the baker's
shovel. A little further on, an apple tree was calling too.

<Dialogue title="The apple tree" setting="The same meadow">
  <Line who="The apples" de="Hallo! Wir sind Äpfel! Wir sind groß und schön!" en="Hello! We're apples! We're big and beautiful!" />
</Dialogue>

The apples were ripe. She shook the tree until every apple had fallen, and piled them up
neatly.

## Frau Holle's bed

At last she came to a little house. An old woman looked out of the window. She had such
big teeth that the girl wanted to run away.

<Dialogue title="The little house" setting="Frau Holle's door">
  <Line who="Frau Holle" de="Guten Tag! Ich bin Frau Holle. Wie heißt du?" en="Good day! I'm Frau Holle. What's your name?" />
  <Line who="Girl" de="Ich heiße Marie." en="My name is Marie." />
  <Line who="Frau Holle" de="Du arbeitest gern, oder?" en="You like working, don't you?" />
  <Line who="Marie" de="Ja, ich arbeite sehr gern." en="Yes, I like working very much." />
  <Line who="Frau Holle" de="Gut! Das Bett, bitte. Oft!" en="Good! The bed, please. Often!" />
</Dialogue>

Her only job was to shake out Frau Holle's bed, thoroughly, so that the feathers flew.
And whenever the feathers flew, it snowed in the world above.

<Dialogue title="Far above" setting="A village, the same morning">
  <Line who="A child" de="Schnee! Schnee!" en="Snow! Snow!" />
</Dialogue>

Marie worked gladly, and Frau Holle was kind to her. But after a while she grew homesick,
even though she was a thousand times better off than at home.

<Dialogue title="Going home" setting="Frau Holle's door">
  <Line who="Frau Holle" de="Du bist fleißig. Das ist gut!" en="You're hard-working. That's good!" />
  <Line who="Marie" de="Danke, Frau Holle!" en="Thank you, Frau Holle!" />
</Dialogue>

Frau Holle led her to a great gate. As Marie stepped through, a shower of gold fell on her
and stuck to her, so that she went home covered in it. On the well sat the rooster, crowing:

<Dialogue title="The well" setting="Back home">
  <Line who="The rooster" de="Kikeriki! Gold! Gold!" en="Cock-a-doodle-doo! Gold! Gold!" />
</Dialogue>

## The other sister

When the mother saw the gold, she wanted the same for her own daughter. So the lazy sister
pricked her finger on purpose, threw the spindle into the well and jumped in after it. She
came to the oven.

<Dialogue title="The oven again" setting="The meadow">
  <Line who="The bread" de="Hallo! Wir sind warm! Wir sind lecker!" en="Hello! We're warm! We're delicious!" />
  <Line who="Lazy sister" de="Nein, danke. Ich arbeite nicht gern." en="No, thanks. I don't like working." />
</Dialogue>

<Dialogue title="The tree again" setting="The meadow">
  <Line who="The apples" de="Hallo! Wir sind groß und schön!" en="Hello! We're big and beautiful!" />
  <Line who="Lazy sister" de="Nein! Ich arbeite nicht gern!" en="No! I don't like working!" />
</Dialogue>

At Frau Holle's she worked hard on the first day, thinking of the gold. On the second day
less, and on the third day she wouldn't get out of bed. She never shook the feathers at all.

<Dialogue title="Frau Holle's door" setting="The third day">
  <Line who="Frau Holle" de="Du bist faul!" en="You're lazy!" />
</Dialogue>

Frau Holle led her to the gate. But instead of gold, a great kettle of black pitch poured
over her.

<Dialogue title="The well" setting="Back home">
  <Line who="The rooster" de="Kikeriki! Gold? Nein! Nein!" en="Cock-a-doodle-doo! Gold? No! No!" />
</Dialogue>

The pitch stuck to her for as long as she lived.

## The real story

**Snow is Frau Holle's work.** When it snows, Germans still say
<span lang="de">*Frau Holle schüttelt die Betten*</span>: Frau Holle is shaking out the
beds. She is older than the Grimms' tale, a figure from folk belief in central Germany,
and the Hoher Meißner, a mountain in northern Hessen, claims her as its own.

**Gold and pitch.** In German retellings the sisters are often called *Goldmarie* and
*Pechmarie*. Those names are later additions, not the Grimms', and so is "Marie": in the Grimms' tale
the girls have no names at all. *Pech* is pitch, the black
tar; it also means bad luck, and *Pech haben* means to be unlucky.
