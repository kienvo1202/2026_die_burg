<Initial>

You know how to ask someone's name. Here is a story where that question is a matter of
life and death. The Brothers Grimm first published it in 1812 and kept polishing it for
decades; this is the later version, the one every German child knows. Read the English to follow the story; the people in it speak German you
already know.

</Initial>

## The straw room

A miller once boasted to the king that his daughter could spin straw into gold. She
couldn't, of course. But the king locked her in a room full of straw, with a spinning
wheel, and told her that by morning the straw had better be gold.

She sat down and cried. Then the door opened, and in came a very small man.

<Dialogue title="The first night" setting="A locked room in the castle, full of straw">
  <Line who="Little man" de="Guten Abend!" en="Good evening!" />
  <Line who="Miller's daughter" de="Guten Abend! Wie heißen Sie?" en="Good evening! What's your name?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Wie bitte?" en="Sorry?" />
  <Line who="Miller's daughter" de="Wie heißen Sie? Woher kommen Sie?" en="What's your name? Where are you from?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Gold, ja? Gold!" en="Gold, yes? Gold!" />
</Dialogue>

Notice what he did. She asked his name, twice, and politely, with
<Say de="Sie" />. He answered with a <Say de="Wie bitte?" /> and changed the subject.
Remember that.

He sat down at the wheel, and by morning the room was full of gold. She paid him with her
necklace. The king, delighted and greedy, filled a bigger room with straw, and the little
man came back and spun it for her ring. On the third night she had nothing left to give.
"Then promise me your first child when you are queen," he said. She promised. Who keeps a
promise like that?

## The queen

The king married her, and a year later she had a beautiful child. She had forgotten all
about the little man. One morning, there he was.

<Dialogue title="A year later" setting="The queen's room, early morning">
  <Line who="Little man" de="Guten Morgen, Frau Königin!" en="Good morning, Your Majesty!" />
  <Line who="Queen" de="Du! Woher kommst du? Wo wohnst du?" en="You! Where do you come from? Where do you live?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Wie heiße ich, Frau Königin? Wie heiße ich?" en="What's my name, Your Majesty? What's my name?" />
</Dialogue>

She offered him every treasure in the kingdom. He wanted the child. But she cried so
much that he gave her one chance: if in three days she could tell him his name, she
could keep her child.

Look at how they talk. He calls her <Say de="Frau Königin" />, much as Aylin called a
stranger <Say de="Herr Huber" />. Yet in the tale both of them say <Say de="du" />: in
fairy tales, almost everyone does.

## Three days of names

On the first day she tried every name she knew. In the Grimms' later editions she
begins with the names of the Three Wise Men (the Grimms spell the third one *Balzer*, an old short
form of Balthasar).

<Dialogue title="The first day" setting="The queen's room">
  <Line who="Queen" de="Du heißt Kaspar, oder?" en="Your name is Kaspar, right?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Nein!" en="No!" />
  <Line who="Queen" de="Melchior?" en="Melchior?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Nein!" en="No!" />
  <Line who="Queen" de="Balthasar?" en="Balthasar?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Nein, nein, nein!" en="No, no, no!" />
</Dialogue>

Overnight she sent a messenger across the land to collect names, and the next day she
had people ask around the neighbourhood for strange ones.

<Dialogue title="The second day" setting="The queen's room, late at night">
  <Line who="Queen" de="Du heißt Rippenbiest, oder?" en="Your name is Rib-beast, right?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Nein!" en="No!" />
  <Line who="Queen" de="Hammelswade?" en="Sheepshanks?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Nein!" en="No!" />
  <Line who="Queen" de="Schnürbein?" en="Lace-leg?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Nein! Gute Nacht, Frau Königin!" en="No! Good night, Your Majesty!" />
</Dialogue>

On the third day the messenger came back with no new names, only a story. By a high
mountain at the edge of the forest, where the fox and the hare say good night to each
other, he had seen a little house with
a fire in front of it. A ridiculous little man was hopping around the fire on one leg,
singing:

<Chunk
  de="Ach, wie gut ist, dass niemand weiß, dass ich Rumpelstilzchen heiß!"
  literal="Oh, how good (it) is that nobody knows that I Rumpelstilzchen am-called!"
  means="Oh, how good that nobody knows my name is Rumpelstilzchen!"
>

You don't need the middle words yet. Look at the last one, though: *heiß* is
<Say de="ich heiße" /> with its final *e* dropped, as Germans often do in speech (and here
it rhymes with *weiß*). It is the verb from lesson 1.

</Chunk>

## The last guess

The little man came for the child. The queen let him wait a little.

<Dialogue title="The third day" setting="The queen's room">
  <Line who="Little man" de="Jetzt, Frau Königin: Wie heiße ich?" en="Now, Your Majesty: what's my name?" />
  <Line who="Queen" de="Du heißt Kunz, oder?" en="Your name is Kunz, right?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Nein!" en="No!" />
  <Line who="Queen" de="Heinz?" en="Heinz?" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Nein!" en="No!" />
  <Line who="Queen" de="Du heißt … Rumpelstilzchen!" en="Your name is… Rumpelstilzchen!" />
  <Line who="Little man" de="Wie …? Woher …?!" en="How…? Where from…?!" />
</Dialogue>

He stamped his foot so hard it went into the ground, and that was the end of him. The
queen kept her child. And the moral, for a German learner: *always* find out what someone
is called.

## The real story

**Names have power.** In many old stories, knowing a creature's true name gives you power
over it. That is why the little man asks *wie heiße ich?* and never answers *wie heißen
Sie?* himself.

**The middle of nowhere.** The servant's forest, *where the fox and the hare say good
night*, is still how Germans describe a place far from anywhere.

**England has the same tale.** In Suffolk he is called *Tom Tit Tot*, and the heroine
guesses his name in much the same way.

**The Grimms changed the ending.** In their first edition of 1812 Rumpelstilzchen simply
runs off in a rage and never comes back. In later editions he stamps his foot into the
ground and tears himself in two.

**du, in tales and today.** In the tale the queen and the little man say
<Say de="du" /> to each other, as nearly everyone in a fairy tale does. Today, meeting a
stranger, you would ask <Say de="Wie heißen Sie?" />.
