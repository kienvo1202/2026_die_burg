<Initial>

You step off the train in Hamburg with a backpack and no German at all. In ten minutes,
at the hostel desk, someone will say a word to you and you will want to say one back. This
lesson gives you that word, the one for the breakfast table the next morning, and the
one for every first meeting after that. Along the way you will find out why German, which
looks so foreign, keeps turning out to be English's close cousin.

</Initial>

Three small scenes, and already a pattern: Jonas and Aylin are the same age in a hostel,
so they say <Say de="du" />. Herr Huber is a stranger, and older, so Aylin says
<Say de="Sie" />. You will meet that choice every day; lesson 2 is about it.

## The insight

<Insight title="German asks how you are called">

In English you ask *what* someone's name is. German asks *how* they are called:
<Say de="Wie heißt du?" en="what's your name?" />. Word for word, that is "how are-called
you?", and it sounds odd until you meet the verb.

<Say de="heißen" /> means "to be called". English had the same word, and lost it. In 1812
Lord Byron introduced the hero of his famous poem like this: "Childe Harold was he
*hight*". *Hight* means "called". It sounded old-fashioned even then, but it is the
English twin of *heißen*. German simply kept it. So when a German asks
<Say de="Wie heißen Sie?" />, they are using the same verb an Englishman would have used a
thousand years ago.

This is not a coincidence. About fifteen hundred years ago, people from the coasts of
what is now northern Germany and Denmark sailed to Britain, and their dialects became
English. One of those peoples, the Angles, gave England its name. They came from Angeln,
a small region about two hours north of Hamburg, where Aylin's train just arrived. That is
why so much German is hiding in plain sight:

- <Say de="Willkommen!" /> and *welcome*
- <Say de="Guten Morgen!" /> and *good morning*
- <Say de="Gute Nacht!" /> and *good night*
- <Say de="ich bin" /> and *I am*: *bin* is a relative of English *be*

You now have two ways to say your name, and both are completely normal:
<Say de="Ich heiße Aylin." en="my name is Aylin" /> and
<Say de="Ich bin Aylin." en="I'm Aylin" />. Here are the two verbs side by side:

| | <span lang="de">*sein*</span> (to be) | <span lang="de">*heißen*</span> (to be called) |
|---|---|---|
| **ich** (I) | <Say de="ich bin" /> | <Say de="ich heiße" /> |
| **du** (you, to a friend) | <Say de="du bist" /> | <Say de="du heißt" /> |
| **Sie** (you, polite) | <Say de="Sie sind" /> | <Say de="Sie heißen" /> |

Look at the ends of the words in the *heißen* column: <span lang="de">*heiß-e*, *heiß-t*, *heiß-en*</span>. The
verb changes its ending to match who you are talking about. That is the idea of
lesson 2, and it unlocks most German verbs.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="More English hiding in German">

Once you know two sound changes, you start seeing English everywhere in German.

**English *gh* is German *ch*.** Both languages once had the same throaty sound. German
kept saying it; English stopped and left the spelling behind as *gh*. So *night* is
<Say de="Nacht" />, *light* is <Say de="Licht" />, *eight* is <Say de="acht" />, and
*daughter* is <Say de="Tochter" />.

**English *y* or *w* often matches German *g*.** *Day* is <Say de="Tag" /> (as in
<Say de="Guten Tag!" />), *way* is <Say de="Weg" />, *yesterday* is <Say de="gestern" />.
And *morrow*, as in *tomorrow*, is <Say de="Morgen" />: the very word in
<Say de="Guten Morgen!" />.

Not every look-alike is a cousin, and a few are traps; the book points them out as they
come. But as a first guess, "what old English word could this be?" works surprisingly
often.

</Deeper>

## Sound: ei and ie

German has two vowel pairs that look alike and sound completely different. The trick:
**say the English name of the second letter.**

- **ei** ends in *i*, so it sounds like the letter *I*, "eye": <Say de="heißen" />
  sounds like "HY-sen".
- **ie** ends in *e*, so it sounds like the letter *E*, "ee": <Say de="Sie" /> sounds
  like "zee", and <Say de="wie" /> like "vee".

<Say de="Auf Wiedersehen!" /> has *ie* (vee-) and then a long *e*, but no *ei*. And did you
notice? German *w* sounds like English *v*, and in most of Germany *s* before a vowel
sounds like *z*. (In the south, in Austria and in Switzerland it is often a sharper *s*:
Herr Huber's <span lang="de">*Sie*</span> sounds more like "see".)

## How people really say it

**Greetings change with the time of day**: <Say de="Guten Morgen!" /> until about ten or
eleven, <Say de="Guten Tag!" /> through the day, <Say de="Guten Abend!" /> from about six.
<Say de="Hallo!" /> works at any hour with almost anyone.

**And with the region.** In Hamburg and the north you will hear <Say de="Moin!" /> all day,
even at midnight. In southern Germany and Austria people say <Say de="Grüß Gott!" />, or
<Say de="Servus!" /> to friends (it means both hi and bye). That is why Herr Huber from
Munich greeted Aylin with <span lang="de">*Grüß Gott*</span> at a breakfast table in Hamburg. In Switzerland,
it is <Say de="Grüezi!" />.

**Greet the room.** In a small shop, a bakery or a doctor's waiting room, Germans say
<Say de="Guten Tag!" /> (or the local version) as they walk in, and
<Say de="Auf Wiedersehen!" /> or <Say de="Tschüss!" /> as they leave. Saying nothing can
come across as rude.

**Surnames in formal settings.** Herr Huber gave his full name, and Aylin answered with
<Say de="Herr Huber" />, not *Franz*. That is normal: in formal settings people are
<Say de="Herr Huber" /> and <Say de="Frau Schneider" /> until they offer their first name.

## Watch out

**<Say de="Gute Nacht!" /> is a goodbye, never a hello.** Say it when you or they are off
to bed, or when you leave late at night. If you *arrive* somewhere at nine in the evening,
say <Say de="Guten Abend!" />.

**ß is not a B.** It is a sharp, hissing *s*, never a *z*: <Say de="heißen" /> is
"HY-sen". (In
Switzerland, ß isn't used at all; the Swiss write *ss*.)

**Don't translate "What is your name?" word for word.** People will understand you, but
it sounds translated from English. Ask the way Germans do:
<Say de="Wie heißt du?" /> or <Say de="Wie heißen Sie?" />.

**Polite *Sie* always has a capital S,** even in the middle of a sentence:
<Say de="Wie heißen Sie?" />. You can't hear it, but you will see it everywhere.
