<Initial>

Siobhan has just moved to Cologne, and she already knows the next five minutes. Someone
will ask her name. She will say it. They will look blank, and she will have to spell it.
This lesson gives her, and you, the German alphabet as Germans say it, every number up
to a hundred, and a way of counting that English had too and quietly gave up.

</Initial>

## Phrases to keep

<Chunk de="Wie ist Ihr Name?" literal="How is your name?" means="What's your name? (formal: forms, desks, the phone)" />

<Chunk de="Wie schreibt man das?" literal="How writes one that?" means="How do you spell that?" />

<Chunk de="Wie alt bist du?" literal="How old are you?" means="How old are you? (polite: Wie alt sind Sie?)" />

<Chunk de="Wie ist deine Handynummer?" literal="How is your handy-number?" means="What's your mobile number? (polite: Wie ist Ihre Handynummer?)" />

In lesson 1 you learned that <span lang="de">*Was ist dein Name?*</span> sounds
translated. <Say de="Wie ist Ihr Name?" /> is different: it is exactly what people say at
a desk or on the phone. Keep these as phrases for now; lesson 7 explains *Ihr*, *Ihre*
and *deine*.

## The insight

<Insight title="German counts like a nursery rhyme">

Sing a song of sixpence, a pocket full of rye: *four-and-twenty blackbirds* baked in a
pie. Or A. E. Housman, in 1896: *When I was one-and-twenty*. That is how German counts,
every day, at every bakery and bus stop:

- <Say de="einundzwanzig" />: one-and-twenty, 21
- <Say de="vierundzwanzig" />: four-and-twenty, 24
- <Say de="zweiundvierzig" />: two-and-forty, 42

English used both orders for centuries and settled on *twenty-one*; units-first now
sounds like a nursery rhyme. German (and Dutch) kept it. So when you hear a German number
over twenty, **wait for the end**: the first thing you hear is the units, and the tens
come last. Write it as one word.

The good news is that you can now build every number to a hundred from the words in
this lesson and three rules:

| | rule | examples |
|---|---|---|
| 13–19 | number + *zehn* | <Say de="dreizehn" />, <Say de="vierzehn" />, <Say de="neunzehn" /> |
| 40–90 | number + *zig* | <Say de="vierzig" />, <Say de="fünfzig" />, <Say de="achtzig" /> |
| 21–99 | units + *und* + tens | <Say de="einundzwanzig" />, <Say de="siebenundfünfzig" /> |

Twenty and thirty you learned as words (<Say de="zwanzig" />, <Say de="dreißig" />); the rule takes over from forty. Four little quirks, and then you have it all:

- **Thirty has ß:** <Say de="dreißig" />, not *dreizig*.
- **Six drops its s:** <Say de="sechzehn" /> and <Say de="sechzig" />, and the *ch* turns soft, as in *ich*.
- **Seven drops its -en:** <Say de="siebzehn" /> and <Say de="siebzig" />.
- **One drops its s before *und*:** <Say de="einundzwanzig" />, not *einsundzwanzig*.

And a hundred is simply <Say de="hundert" />.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="Why say zwo?">

On a crackly phone line, <span lang="de">*zwei*</span> and <span lang="de">*drei*</span>
sound alarmingly alike: same vowel, same ending. So German has a second word for two,
<Say de="zwo" />, an old form that never quite went away. You hear it when people read
out phone numbers, on the radio, in the army, and from anyone who wants to be sure. It is
always fine to say <span lang="de">*zwei*</span>; just don't be thrown when you hear
<span lang="de">*zwo*</span>.

</Deeper>

## Sound: spelling aloud

German spells with letter names that are close to English, with a handful of traps.
These are the ones that catch English speakers:

| letter | sounds like | | letter | sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | "ah" | | **J** | "yot" |
| **E** | "ay" | | **Q** | "koo" |
| **I** | "ee" | | **V** | "fow" (rhymes with *cow*) |
| **G** | "gay" | | **W** | "vay" |
| **H** | "hah" | | **Y** | "ÜP-see-lon" |
| **U** | "oo" | | **Z** | "tset" |

**E and I are the big one:** German E sounds like the English letter A, and German I like the English letter E. When Siobhan
spells <span lang="de">K, E, L, L, Y</span>, her E sounds like an English A.

The extra letters have names too: <Say de="Ä" />, <Say de="Ö" />, <Say de="Ü" /> (say the
sound), and ß is <Say de="Eszett" />, "ess-tset".

## How people really say it

**Spelling on the phone.** For a name like Kelly, Germans use a spelling alphabet:
<span lang="de">*K wie Kaufmann*</span>, "K as in Kaufmann". The traditional one uses first
names, and many people still say <span lang="de">*A wie Anton, B wie Berta*</span>. Since
2022 the official standard uses city names instead, like <span lang="de">*B wie Berlin*</span>
and <span lang="de">*H wie Hamburg*</span>. You will hear both; either works.

**Phone numbers come in pairs.** After the area code, people often read numbers two
digits at a time, as Siobhan did: <Say de="zweiundvierzig, achtzehn" />. Mobile numbers
start with <span lang="de">*015*</span>, <span lang="de">*016*</span> or
<span lang="de">*017*</span>.

**Handwriting.** A German 1 has a long upstroke, so it can look like an English 7. A line
through the 7 keeps the two apart.

**Fingers.** Germans count on their fingers starting with the thumb. Show three the
English way, with index, middle and ring finger, and you give yourself away as a foreigner.
Fans of *Inglourious Basterds* will remember a scene that turns on exactly this.

**Two numbers to know:** <Say de="eins, eins, zwei" /> (112) for an ambulance or the fire
brigade, <Say de="eins, eins, null" /> (110) for the police.

## Watch out

**21 is einundzwanzig, not "zwanzigeins".** Units first, then *und*, then the tens, all
in one word.

**E and I swap.** Spelling your name, the German E sounds like an English A, and the
German I like an English E.

**Age goes with sein:** <Say de="Ich bin vierunddreißig." /> (I *am* thirty-four), or
<Say de="Ich bin vierunddreißig Jahre alt." />. Never "Ich habe vierunddreißig Jahre",
which is what French and Spanish speakers often try.

**Handy is German.** <Say de="das Handy" /> is the everyday word for a mobile phone. It
looks English, but English speakers don't use it that way.
