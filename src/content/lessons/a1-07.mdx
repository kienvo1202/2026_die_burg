<Initial>

Show someone a photo of your family and the conversation writes itself: who's that, how
old is she, where do they live? This lesson gives you the words for the people in the
picture and the little words that say whose they are. One of those words was in your
grandparents' Bible, and German still uses it every day.

</Initial>

Jana's family was <Say de="meine Familie" />, Paul's daughter was <Say de="seine Tochter" />,
and Oma asked Kofi about <Say de="Ihre Familie" />. And one word, *Freund*, nearly caused
a family incident. Let's untangle all of it.

## Phrases to keep

<Chunk de="Wer ist das?" literal="Who is that?" means="Who's that?" />

<Chunk de="Wie alt ist er?" literal="How old is he?" means="How old is he?" />

<Chunk de="Das sind meine Eltern." literal="That are my parents." means="These are my parents." />

<Chunk de="Hast du Geschwister?" literal="Have you siblings?" means="Do you have brothers or sisters?" />

<span lang="de">*Geschwister*</span> means brothers and sisters together, one handy word where
English says *brothers and sisters* or *siblings*. Lesson 13 shows why the verb comes first in that question.

## The insight

<Insight title="Mine and thine">

"Our Father, which art in heaven, hallowed be *thy* name." Four hundred years ago,
English had two kinds of "your": *thy* and *thine* for one person close to you (or God,
as lesson 2 said), and *your* for everyone else. *Thine* is German <Say de="dein" />, and *mine* is
<Say de="mein" />. Same words, same jobs: <Say de="mein Vater" />, my father;
<Say de="dein Vater" />, your father.

They take the same endings as <span lang="de">*ein*</span> from lesson 4: nothing for
*der* and *das* words, *-e* for *die* words, and *-e* for plurals.

| | *a* | *my* | *your* (friend) |
|---|---|---|---|
| <span lang="de">*der*</span> words | <Say de="ein Bruder" /> | <Say de="mein Bruder" /> | <Say de="dein Bruder" /> |
| <span lang="de">*die*</span> words | <Say de="eine Schwester" /> | <Say de="meine Schwester" /> | <Say de="deine Schwester" /> |
| <span lang="de">*das*</span> words | <Say de="ein Kind" /> | <Say de="mein Kind" /> | <Say de="dein Kind" /> |
| plurals | | <Say de="meine Eltern" /> | <Say de="deine Eltern" /> |

That finally explains lesson 3: <Say de="Wie ist Ihr Name?" /> is *der Name*, so no
ending; <Say de="Wie ist Ihre Handynummer?" /> is *die Handynummer*, from *die Nummer*, so *-e*. *Ihr* with a
capital is the polite *your*, the partner of *Sie*.

**Now the trap English speakers fall into.** *His* is <Say de="sein" />, *her* is
<Say de="ihr" />. You choose the word by the **owner**, as in English. But the ending
follows the **thing owned**, as in German:

- Paul and his daughter: <Say de="seine Tochter" /> (*sein*, because Paul; *-e*, because *die Tochter*)
- Ama and her husband: <Say de="ihr Mann" /> (*ihr*, because Ama; no ending, because *der Mann*)

So *seine* can mean "his" with a feminine ending, and that is not a contradiction: the
ending belongs to the daughter, not to Paul.

**The rest of the family:** <Say de="unser" /> (our) and <Say de="euer" /> (your, to
friends) work the same way: <span lang="de">*unsere Oma*</span>,
<span lang="de">*eure Eltern*</span>. (*Euer* loses an *e* when an ending is added.)

</Insight>

<Deeper title="haben, the second truly irregular verb">

<span lang="de">*Haben*</span>, to have, is almost regular, but it loses its *b* in the
*du* and *er* forms:

| | |
|---|---|
| **ich** | <Say de="ich habe" /> |
| **du** | <Say de="du hast" /> |
| **er · sie · es** | <Say de="er hat" /> |
| **wir** | <Say de="wir haben" /> |
| **ihr** | <Say de="ihr habt" /> |
| **sie · Sie** | <Say de="sie haben" /> |

*Hast* and *hat* are English's old *hast* and *hath*: "thou hast", "he hath". You now
have the two verbs German leans on most, *sein* and *haben*.

</Deeper>

## Sound: the endings you mustn't swallow

German endings are short, but they are there. A final **-e** is a quick "uh", never
silent: <Say de="meine" /> is "MY-nuh", <Say de="ich habe" /> ends "HAH-buh". If you drop
it, *meine* sounds like *mein*, and the ending you just learned disappears.

A final **-en** shrinks in everyday speech to a light *n*: <Say de="haben" /> sounds like
"HAH-bn", <Say de="wohnen" /> like "VOH-nn".

## How people really say it

**Freund or ein Freund?** <span lang="de">*Mein Freund*</span> usually means boyfriend, and
<span lang="de">*meine Freundin*</span> often means girlfriend, especially when a man says it. For a friend,
Germans say <Say de="ein Freund" /> or <span lang="de">*eine Freundin*</span>, or name the
person. That is why Jana corrected her grandmother so fast.

**Frau and Mann** also mean wife and husband: <Say de="seine Frau" /> is his wife,
<Say de="ihr Mann" /> her husband.

**Oma and Opa** are the everyday words for grandma and grandpa, used by adults too.

**Small families.** Most German families have one or two children, and many people have
no brothers or sisters. Ask <Say de="Hast du Geschwister?" /> and "no" is a common answer.

**Photos are for friends.** Showing family photos to colleagues you have just met is
unusual; at a friend's kitchen table on a Sunday, over *Kaffee und Kuchen*, it is
completely normal.

## Watch out

**His or her: look at the owner.** Ama and her husband is <Say de="ihr Mann" />, never
"sein Mann", even though *der Mann* is a *der* word.

**The ending: look at the thing.** <span lang="de">*Mein Bruder*</span>, but
<span lang="de">*meine Schwester*</span> and <span lang="de">*meine Eltern*</span>.

**Three ihrs, so far.** <span lang="de">*ihr*</span> (her), <span lang="de">*ihr*</span> (you,
friends, from lesson 6) and <span lang="de">*Ihr*</span> (your, polite). The sentence tells
you which.

**Das sind for more than one.** <Say de="Das sind meine Eltern." />, not "Das ist meine
Eltern".

**Eltern is always plural.** For one parent, just say <span lang="de">*meine Mutter*</span> or
<span lang="de">*mein Vater*</span>.
