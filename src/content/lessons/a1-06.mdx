<Initial>

Small talk in any language runs out fast, unless you can say what you like. Once you can,
a conversation has somewhere to go. This lesson gives you the most useful little word in
German small talk, and it turns out English had it all along, hidden in a word you only
use for longing.

</Initial>

## The scene

<Dialogue title="Tandem" setting="A language café in Vienna, Thursday evening">
  <Line who="Felix" de="Servus! Ich bin Felix." en="Hi! I'm Felix." />
  <Line who="Priya" de="Hallo! Ich heiße Priya. Ich komme aus Indien." en="Hello! My name's Priya. I'm from India." />
  <Line who="Felix" de="Was machst du gern, Priya?" en="What do you like doing, Priya?" />
  <Line who="Priya" de="Ich lese gern, und ich koche sehr gern. Und du?" en="I like reading, and I really like cooking. And you?" />
  <Line who="Felix" de="Ich lese auch gern! Und ich wandere gern, und ich spiele Gitarre." en="I like reading too! And I like hiking, and I play the guitar." />
  <Line who="Priya" de="Du wanderst oft, oder?" en="You hike often, right?" />
  <Line who="Felix" de="Ja, sehr oft. Ich mache gern Sport. Und du?" en="Yes, very often. I like doing sport. And you?" />
  <Line who="Priya" de="Sport? Nein, danke! Ich wandere nicht gern." en="Sport? No, thanks! I don't like hiking." />
  <Line who="Felix" de="Und Musik?" en="And music?" />
  <Line who="Priya" de="Ich höre sehr gern Musik. Und ich singe … manchmal!" en="I love listening to music. And I sing… sometimes!" />
  <Line who="Felix" de="Und ich tanze nicht gern." en="And I don't like dancing." />
  <Line who="Priya" de="Ich auch nicht!" en="Me neither!" />
</Dialogue>

<Dialogue title="Friends arrive" setting="The same table, half an hour later">
  <Line who="Felix" de="Hallo, Anna! Hallo, Moritz! Das ist Priya." en="Hi, Anna! Hi, Moritz! This is Priya." />

  <Line who="Priya" de="Mich auch! Was macht ihr gern?" en="Nice to meet you too! What do you two like doing?" />
  <Line who="Moritz" de="Wir spielen gern Fußball, und wir schwimmen gern." en="We like playing football, and we like swimming." />

  <Line who="Felix" de="Sie spielen Fußball, sie reisen … aber sie kochen nicht gern!" en="They play football, they travel… but they don't like cooking!" />

</Dialogue>

Count how often you heard <Say de="gern" />. Priya never said "I like". She said
*I read gladly*. That is today's idea.

## The insight

<Insight title="Gern is English yearn">

The everyday German way to say "I like doing" isn't a verb at all. German does the verb,
and adds one small word after it: <Say de="gern" />, *gladly*.

- <Say de="Ich lese." />: I read.
- <Say de="Ich lese gern." />: I read gladly. I like reading.
- <Say de="Ich lese nicht gern." />: I don't read gladly. I don't like reading.

Any verb works: <Say de="Ich koche gern." />, <Say de="Wir spielen gern Fußball." />
Add <Say de="sehr" /> to make it stronger: <Say de="Ich koche sehr gern." />

And *gern* is not foreign at all: it is a cousin of English **yearn**. Old English had
*georn*, eager, keen, and from the same root the verb that became *yearn*, to desire.
English kept the longing; German kept the everyday sense. What you do *gern* is what you do eagerly, gladly. So when a German says <span lang="de">*Ich wandere gern*</span>,
they are saying, in a way, "I yearn to hike", only much more casually.

**Where it goes:** after the verb, never before it: <span lang="de">*Ich lese gern*</span>,
never "Ich gern lese". Little words can come in between (<span lang="de">*Ich lese auch
gern*</span>, <span lang="de">*sehr gern*</span>), and in a question *gern* follows the
person: <span lang="de">*Was machst du gern?*</span>

**And the verb table is now complete.** In lesson 2 you had *ich*, *du* and *Sie*;
lesson 5 added *er*, *sie*, *es*. Here are the rest:

| | <span lang="de">*spielen*</span> | <span lang="de">*sein*</span> |
|---|---|---|
| **ich** | <Say de="ich spiele" /> | <Say de="ich bin" /> |
| **du** | <Say de="du spielst" /> | <Say de="du bist" /> |
| **er · sie · es** | <Say de="er spielt" /> | <Say de="er ist" /> |
| **wir** (we) | <Say de="wir spielen" /> | <Say de="wir sind" /> |
| **ihr** (you, friends) | <Say de="ihr spielt" /> | <Say de="ihr seid" /> |
| **sie** (they) · **Sie** | <Say de="sie spielen" /> | <Say de="sie sind" /> |

Notice the gift: *wir*, *sie* and *Sie* all use the dictionary form, for every verb except
*sein*. Every regular verb in German follows this table (with the small s-sound twist from
lesson 2: <span lang="de">*du tanzt*</span>), and you now know all of it.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="Gern, lieber, am liebsten">

*Gern* has a comparative, like *good, better, best*: <span lang="de">*Ich lese gern, ich
koche lieber, und ich reise am liebsten*</span>: I like reading, I prefer cooking, and
best of all I like travelling. A later lesson covers comparing; for now, recognise it when
you hear it.

</Deeper>

## Sound: the German r

At the start of a syllable, German *r* is made at the back of the throat, a soft gargle
rather than an English *r*: <Say de="reisen" />, <Say de="Priya" />. In Switzerland and parts of Bavaria and Austria it is often rolled on
the tongue instead; both are correct.

At the end of a word, *r* hardly sounds at all. *-er* becomes a short, open "a"
(<Say de="wandern" />), and after a long vowel the *r* melts into a soft "a":
<Say de="wir" /> ("veer-a"), <Say de="hier" />. English speakers tend to
add a full English *r*; leave it out and you sound far more German.

## How people really say it

**A country of clubs.** Germans organise their hobbies: sports clubs, choirs, hiking
clubs, chess clubs, allotment clubs. Around 600,000 clubs (*Vereine*) are registered in
Germany alone. Joining one is the classic way to make friends in a new town.

**Wandern** is more than a walk: boots, a route, often a stop at a mountain hut. In
Austria, Switzerland and southern Germany it is a weekend ritual.

**Tandem.** A <span lang="de">*Sprachcafé*</span> or a tandem pairs people who want to
swap languages: half an hour in German, half an hour in English. It is free, and with
this lesson you have enough to start.

**Servus** is how Felix and Anna greet in Vienna, between friends: you met it in lesson 1.

## Watch out

**gern goes after the verb:** <Say de="Ich spiele gern Gitarre." />, never "Ich gern
spiele".

**Not "Ich mag lesen".** It is understood, but sounds odd. For activities, use
<span lang="de">*gern*</span>.

**ihr is not Sie.** <Say de="ihr" /> means you, to two or more friends. Polite *you* is
<span lang="de">*Sie*</span>, whether to one person or many.

**sie or Sie?** <span lang="de">*sie spielen*</span> (they play) and
<span lang="de">*Sie spielen*</span> (you play, polite) sound identical. In writing the
capital tells them apart, except at the start of a sentence; in speech, only the situation
does.

**Ich auch, or Ich auch nicht?** Agree with "I like…" using <Say de="Ich auch!" />, and
with "I don't like…" using <Say de="Ich auch nicht." />
