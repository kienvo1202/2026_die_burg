<Initial>

Every first day somewhere new comes down to three questions. Who are you? Where are you
from? And do we have a language in common? You can already answer the first. By the end
of this lesson you will answer the other two, and ask them back, politely or as a friend.
You will also find a question that English and German once asked in exactly the same
way.

</Initial>

## The scene

Read the German first and guess. Then listen, then check the English.

<Dialogue title="First lesson" setting="A language school in Leipzig, nine in the morning">
  <Line who="Frau Schneider" de="Guten Morgen und willkommen! Ich bin Anna Schneider." en="Good morning and welcome! I'm Anna Schneider." />
  <Line who="Frau Schneider" de="Wie heißen Sie?" en="What's your name?" />
  <Line who="Diego" de="Ich heiße Diego Ruiz." en="My name is Diego Ruiz." />
  <Line who="Frau Schneider" de="Woher kommen Sie, Herr Ruiz?" en="Where are you from, Mr Ruiz?" />
  <Line who="Diego" de="Ich komme aus Mexiko. Aus Puebla." en="I'm from Mexico. From Puebla." />
  <Line who="Frau Schneider" de="Und wo wohnen Sie jetzt?" en="And where do you live now?" />
  <Line who="Diego" de="Ich wohne jetzt in Leipzig." en="I live in Leipzig now." />
  <Line who="Frau Schneider" de="Sprechen Sie Englisch?" en="Do you speak English?" />
  <Line who="Diego" de="Ja, und natürlich Spanisch." en="Yes, and Spanish, of course." />
</Dialogue>

<Dialogue title="Coffee break" setting="The same school, an hour later, by the coffee machine">
  <Line who="Hana" de="Hallo! Du bist Diego, oder?" en="Hi! You're Diego, right?" />
  <Line who="Diego" de="Ja! Und du bist …?" en="Yes! And you are…?" />
  <Line who="Hana" de="Ich heiße Hana." en="My name's Hana." />
  <Line who="Diego" de="Freut mich! Woher kommst du?" en="Nice to meet you! Where are you from?" />
  <Line who="Hana" de="Wie bitte?" en="Sorry?" />
  <Line who="Diego" de="Woher kommst du?" en="Where are you from?" />
  <Line who="Hana" de="Ah! Aus Japan. Aus Osaka." en="Oh! From Japan. From Osaka." />
  <Line who="Hana" de="Sprichst du Englisch?" en="Do you speak English?" />
  <Line who="Diego" de="Ja, Englisch und Spanisch. Ich spreche nur ein bisschen Deutsch." en="Yes, English and Spanish. I only speak a little German." />
  <Line who="Hana" de="Ich auch!" en="Me too!" />
</Dialogue>

Diego says <Say de="Sie" /> to his teacher and <Say de="du" /> to Hana, and so does
everyone else in the room. Listen to what that does to the verbs:
<Say de="Woher kommen Sie?" /> but <Say de="Woher kommst du?" />. That change is today's
idea.

## Phrases to keep

<Chunk de="Woher kommst du?" literal="Where-from come you?" means="Where are you from?" />

<Chunk de="Ich spreche ein bisschen Deutsch." literal="I speak a little-bit German." means="I speak a little German." />

<Chunk de="…, oder?" literal="…, or?" means="…, right?" />

<Chunk de="Sprechen Sie Englisch?" literal="Speak you English?" means="Do you speak English?" />

That last one puts the verb first to ask a yes-or-no question. Keep it as a phrase for
now; lesson 13 shows how to build any question like it.

## The insight

<Insight title="The ending tells you who">

In English, the verb hardly cares who is doing it: *I come, you come, they come*. In
German, every verb changes its ending to match the person. Here are
<span lang="de">*kommen*</span> (to come) and <span lang="de">*wohnen*</span> (to live):

| | <span lang="de">*kommen*</span> | <span lang="de">*wohnen*</span> |
|---|---|---|
| **ich** (-e) | <Say de="ich komme" /> | <Say de="ich wohne" /> |
| **du** (-st) | <Say de="du kommst" /> | <Say de="du wohnst" /> |
| **Sie** (-en) | <Say de="Sie kommen" /> | <Say de="Sie wohnen" /> |

So a German sentence tells you who twice: once with the pronoun, once with the ending.
That sounds like extra work, until you notice English did exactly the same thing not so
long ago. Open a King James Bible at the Book of Job, and God asks Satan:

> *"Whence comest thou?"*

*Whence* means "where from". *Comest* is *come* with the du-ending. *Thou* is *du*. Now
read it in modern German:

<Chunk de="Woher kommst du?" literal="Whence comest thou?" means="Where are you from?" />

It translates word for word: question word, verb with its du-ending, then *thou*. German
didn't add the endings; English dropped them.

The good news is that the same recipe works for most verbs. Take the dictionary form,
which usually ends in *-en*: <Say de="wohnen" />. Remove the *-en* to get the stem,
<span lang="de">*wohn-*</span>. Add the ending for the person: <Say de="ich wohne" />,
<Say de="du wohnst" />, <Say de="Sie wohnen" />. And notice the gift in the last line: the
polite *Sie* form is simply the dictionary form, for every verb except
<Say de="sein" /> (<Say de="Sie sind" />). Whenever you are unsure, polite German is the
easy German.

You already know the two verbs that don't follow the recipe completely:
<Say de="sein" /> (<span lang="de">*bin, bist, sind*</span>) is irregular in German just
as *am, are, is* are in English. And <Say de="heißen" /> is almost regular:
<Say de="du heißt" />, not "heißst", because the stem already ends in an *s*-sound and
German doesn't stack two of them.

One verb in today's scene breaks the pattern: <Say de="du sprichst" />, not "du
sprechst". A few dozen common verbs change their vowel in the du-form. Take
<Say de="Sprichst du Englisch?" /> as a phrase for now; a later lesson shows the pattern
behind it.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="English had du and Sie too">

English once made the same choice German makes every day. *Thou* was the familiar form,
for friends, family, children and God. *You* was the respectful form for a single
person, and also the plural, just as German *Sie* is today.

Over the 1600s, English speakers used the polite *you* with more and more people, until
*thou* sounded either intimate, rude or religious, and disappeared from most everyday
speech.
English solved the du-or-Sie problem by being polite to everyone. German kept both, which
is why you now have to choose.

</Deeper>

## Sound: the two ch sounds

German *ch* has two sounds, and the vowel in front decides which one.

- **After a, o, u and au** it is the throaty sound at the back of the mouth, like Scottish
  *loch*: <Say de="auch" />.
- **Everywhere else**, after *i, e, ä, ö, ü* and after consonants, it is a soft hiss
  made at the front of the mouth. Say the *h* in *huge* and stretch it:
  <Say de="ich" />, <Say de="sprechen" />, <Say de="natürlich" />, <Say de="Österreich" />.

Listen to <Say de="Ich auch!" />: both sounds in two words, the soft one first.

## How people really say it

**Choosing between *du* and *Sie*.** Use <Say de="Sie" /> with strangers, shop staff,
officials, older people, and at work until someone offers you <Say de="du" />. Use
<Say de="du" /> with friends, family, children, fellow students and people your age in
casual places, and online. Many younger workplaces use *du* from day one. When you can't
tell, use *Sie*: being too polite costs nothing, and the other person can always offer
*du*. It is usually the older or more senior person who offers.

**Language classes are a good example.** Teachers often say *Sie* to adult students,
while students say *du* to each other, exactly as in today's scene.

**German is not only German.** It is spoken in <Say de="Deutschland" />,
<Say de="Österreich" /> and <Say de="die Schweiz" /> (where French, Italian and Romansh are
spoken too), in Liechtenstein, Luxembourg and eastern Belgium, and in South Tyrol in
northern Italy. If
someone says <Say de="Ich komme aus Österreich." />, expect *Servus* rather than *Moin*.

**A little German opens doors.** Starting with
<Say de="Ich spreche nur ein bisschen Deutsch." /> tells people to slow down, and most
will.

## Watch out

**Where is *wo*; where from is *woher*.** You live <Say de="in" /> a place and come
<Say de="aus" /> a place: <Say de="Wo wohnst du?" />, but <Say de="Woher kommst du?" />.
"Wo kommst du?" on its own is a very common learner mistake. (You will also hear
<span lang="de">*Wo kommst du her?*</span>: that is *woher* split in two, and perfectly
good German.)

**Match the ending to the person, not to the last word you heard.** The question was
<Say de="Woher kommen Sie?" />, but you answer for yourself: <Say de="Ich komme aus…" />,
never "ich kommen".

**<Say de="Mich auch!" /> or <Say de="Ich auch!" />?** Mirror the sentence you are
agreeing with. *Freut mich!* has *mich* in it, so you answer *Mich auch!*.
*Ich spreche nur ein bisschen Deutsch* starts with *ich*, so you answer *Ich auch!*.

**A few countries come with an article.** Most don't (<Say de="aus Mexiko" />,
<Say de="aus Österreich" />), but Switzerland does, and the article changes after *aus*:
<Say de="aus der Schweiz" />. Learn that one as a phrase; lesson 16 explains why.
