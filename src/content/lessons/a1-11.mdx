<Initial>

In a clothes shop you keep pointing at things: this jacket, that jumper, these shoes. To
say "I'll take it", German wants to know which "it" you mean, and the answer is a word
you already almost know. This lesson takes you shopping in Basel, in Switzerland, for a
first winter coat.

</Initial>

## The scene

<Dialogue title="The jacket" setting="A second-hand shop in Basel, a cold Saturday in November. Noor is from Amman and new in Basel; Luca is from Basel.">
  <Line who="Frau Meier" de="Grüezi!" en="Hello!" />
  <Line who="Noor" de="Grüezi! Ich suche eine Jacke." en="Hello! I'm looking for a jacket." />
  <Line who="Frau Meier" de="Welche Größe haben Sie?" en="What size are you?" />
  <Line who="Noor" de="Achtunddreißig." en="Thirty-eight." />

  <Line who="Noor" de="Sie ist schön, aber sie ist zu groß." en="It's nice, but it's too big." />
  <Line who="Frau Meier" de="Hier, eine Jacke in Größe sechsunddreißig." en="Here, a jacket in a thirty-six." />
  <Line who="Noor" de="Sie passt!" en="It fits!" />
  <Line who="Luca" de="Das steht dir! Aber grau? Gibt es die Jacke auch in Schwarz?" en="It suits you! But grey? Does the jacket come in black too?" />
  <Line who="Frau Meier" de="Ja, hier: auch sechsunddreißig, in Schwarz." en="Yes, here: also a thirty-six, in black." />
  <Line who="Noor" de="Gut, ich nehme sie." en="Good, I'll take it." />
</Dialogue>

<Dialogue title="At the till" setting="The same shop, ten minutes later">
  <Line who="Luca" de="Und der Pullover hier? Er ist blau und nicht teuer." en="And this jumper? It's blue and not expensive." />
  <Line who="Noor" de="Blau … ja! Ich nehme ihn." en="Blue… yes! I'll take it." />
  <Line who="Luca" de="Und das Hemd ist schön. Ich nehme es!" en="And the shirt is nice. I'll take it!" />
  <Line who="Frau Meier" de="Die Jacke und der Pullover: fünfzig Franken. Bar oder mit Karte?" en="The jacket and the jumper: fifty francs. Cash or card?" />
  <Line who="Noor" de="Mit Karte, bitte." en="By card, please." />
</Dialogue>

Three times someone says "I'll take it", and it's three different words:
<Say de="Ich nehme sie" /> for the jacket, <Say de="Ich nehme ihn" /> for the jumper,
<Say de="Ich nehme es" /> for the shirt. Each one matches its noun.

## Phrases to keep

<Chunk de="Kann ich … anprobieren?" literal="Can I … on-try?" means="Can I try … on?" />

<Chunk de="Welche Größe haben Sie?" literal="Which size have you?" means="What size are you?" />

<Chunk de="Wie gefällt dir …?" literal="How pleases (to) you …?" means="How do you like …?" />

<Chunk de="Das steht dir!" literal="That stands (to) you!" means="That suits you!" />

<Chunk de="Bar oder mit Karte?" literal="Cash or with card?" means="Cash or card?" />

*Kann ich* is a verb from lesson 12. And *dir* in *gefällt dir* and *steht dir* means
"to you": take both as phrases for now.

## The insight

<Insight title="ihn: the -n of den comes back">

You already have both halves of today's rule.

- **Lesson 5:** a thing is *er*, *sie* or *es* by its article. *Der Pullover* is a "he".
- **Lesson 8:** when a *der* word is the thing you have, need or take, it changes:
  *der* becomes *den*. *die* and *das* stay the same.

Put them together and you get the word for "it" when you take, buy or try on a thing:

| the thing | doing | taken, bought, tried on |
|---|---|---|
| <Say de="der Pullover" /> | <Say de="er" /> | <Say de="ihn" /> |
| <Say de="die Jacke" /> | <Say de="sie" /> | <Say de="sie" /> |
| <Say de="das Hemd" /> | <Say de="es" /> | <Say de="es" /> |
| <Say de="die Schuhe" /> | <Say de="sie" /> | <Say de="sie" /> |

One new word, and only for *der* words: **ihn**. Listen to its ending. The *-n* of
*den* turns up again: <Say de="den Pullover" /> → <Say de="ihn" />.

- <Say de="Der Pullover ist blau. Ich nehme ihn." />
- <Say de="Die Jacke passt. Ich nehme sie." />

**English had this too.** Old English had two forms where modern English has one *him*:
*hine* for him as the one something is done to ("I see him"), and *him* for "to him".
German still has both endings: *ihn*, and *ihm*, which you'll meet later.
English dropped *hine* and let *him* do both jobs. The words are cousins rather than
twins (German *er* and English *he* grew from different roots), but the endings match:
*-n* for the thing done to, *-m* for "to".

And because *der Pullover* is a "he", *ihn* means "it" as well as "him". In a German shop
you keep saying, in effect, "I'll take *him*". It sounds like a love story. It's a jumper.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="Colours, sizes and zu">

Colours work like every adjective after *sein* (lesson 5): they never change.
<Say de="Die Jacke ist schwarz." /> <Say de="Der Mantel ist braun." />
<Say de="Das T-Shirt ist weiß." />

After *in* on its own, with no noun following, a colour is a noun and takes a capital:
<Say de="Gibt es die Jacke auch in Schwarz?" /> *in Rot*, *in Grün*, *in Weiß*.

*zu* before an adjective means "too": <Say de="Die Hose ist zu lang." />
<Say de="Das Kleid ist zu kurz." />

Putting a colour straight before a noun ("the black jacket") needs endings; that comes
in A1.2.

</Deeper>

## Sound: au, eu, ei

Three vowel pairs glide from one sound to another:

- **au** is "ow": <Say de="blau" />, <Say de="grau" />, <Say de="braun" />, <Say de="kaufen" />.
- **eu** and **äu** are "oy": <Say de="teuer" />, <Say de="heute" />.
- **ei** is "eye" (lesson 1): <Say de="weiß" />.

Three colours in a row, *blau, grau, braun*, are good practice.

## How people really say it

**Switzerland writes without ß.** In <Say de="die Schweiz" />, *Größe* is spelled
*Grösse* and *weiß* is *weiss*. Same words, same sounds; Swiss Standard German simply
uses *ss* everywhere. This course keeps the German spelling, even in Basel.

**Francs, not euros.** Prices are in Swiss francs (CHF, <Say de="der Franken" />), with 100
*Rappen* to the franc.

**Two kinds of German.** German-speaking Swiss speak Swiss German dialect among
themselves and write Standard German; they switch to Standard German when speaking with
people who don't know the dialect. <Say de="Grüezi!" /> is the polite hello you'll hear across German-speaking Switzerland.

**Sizes** are European (36, 38, 40) or S, M, L. Second-hand shops, called *Brockenhaus*
or *Brocki* in Switzerland, are popular and cheap.

## Watch out

**der Mantel is ihn, not es.** The pronoun follows the article, every time:
<Say de="Der Mantel? Ich nehme ihn." />

**die Hose is one pair.** <Say de="die Hose" /> means one pair of trousers;
<Say de="die Hosen" /> are several pairs.

**passen or stehen?** <Say de="Die Jacke passt." /> is about size: it fits.
<Say de="Das steht dir!" /> is about looks: it suits you.

**sie can mean many things.** "she", "her", "it" for a *die* word, "they" or "them",
and, with a capital, polite "you": <Say de="Die Schuhe? Ich nehme sie." />
<Say de="Welche Größe haben Sie?" /> The sentence tells you which.
