<Initial>

German time has a trap in it that has made more than one British visitor an hour late.
This lesson gives you the clock, the days of the week and the words for booking a
hairdresser or finding a free hour with a friend. And it shows you the rule that decides
where every German verb stands.

</Initial>

## The scene

<Dialogue title="On the phone" setting="Frankfurt, Tuesday, nine o'clock. Ana, from São Paulo, phones a hair salon from her office.">
  <Line who="Frau Schmitt" de="Friseur Schmitt, guten Morgen!" en="Schmitt's hair salon, good morning!" />
  <Line who="Ana" de="Guten Morgen! Hier ist Ana Costa. Ich möchte einen Termin." en="Good morning! This is Ana Costa. I'd like an appointment." />
  <Line who="Frau Schmitt" de="Gern. Am Donnerstag um zehn Uhr?" en="Of course. Thursday at ten?" />
  <Line who="Ana" de="Am Donnerstag arbeite ich. Am Freitag? Geht das?" en="On Thursday I'm working. Friday? Does that work?" />
  <Line who="Frau Schmitt" de="Am Freitag … um halb drei ist noch frei." en="Friday… half past two is still free." />
  <Line who="Ana" de="Halb drei: Das ist vierzehn Uhr dreißig, oder?" en="Half past two: that's 14:30, right?" />
  <Line who="Frau Schmitt" de="Ja, vierzehn Uhr dreißig." en="Yes, 14:30." />
  <Line who="Ana" de="Gut, am Freitag um halb drei. Danke! Auf Wiederhören!" en="Good, Friday at half past two. Thanks! Goodbye!" />
</Dialogue>

<Dialogue title="Finding an hour" setting="The same office, a quarter of an hour later">
  <Line who="Ana" de="Jonas, wie spät ist es?" en="Jonas, what time is it?" />
  <Line who="Jonas" de="Viertel nach neun. Hast du heute Zeit? Kaffee?" en="Quarter past nine. Are you free today? Coffee?" />
  <Line who="Ana" de="Heute nicht. Und morgen auch nicht." en="Not today. And not tomorrow either." />
  <Line who="Jonas" de="Wann hast du Zeit? Am Donnerstag?" en="When are you free? Thursday?" />
  <Line who="Ana" de="Am Donnerstag um zehn habe ich einen Termin. Aber um zwölf habe ich Zeit." en="On Thursday at ten I've got a meeting. But at twelve I'm free." />
  <Line who="Jonas" de="Um zwölf habe ich keine Zeit. Am Montag um Viertel nach drei? Geht das?" en="At twelve I can't. Monday at quarter past three? Does that work?" />
  <Line who="Ana" de="Ja, das geht. Am Montag um Viertel nach drei!" en="Yes, that works. Monday at quarter past three!" />
  <Line who="Jonas" de="Bis dann!" en="See you then!" />
</Dialogue>

Two things to listen for. Ana checks *halb drei* against the 24-hour clock, because it
isn't what an English speaker expects. And nobody says "Am Donnerstag ich arbeite": the
verb jumps ahead of *ich*.

## Phrases to keep

<Chunk de="Wie spät ist es?" literal="How late is it?" means="What time is it?" />

<Chunk de="Hast du am … Zeit?" literal="Have you on-the … time?" means="Are you free on …?" />

<Chunk de="Geht das?" literal="Goes that?" means="Does that work for you?" />

<Chunk de="Bis dann!" literal="Until then!" means="See you then!" />

*Bis* works with any day: <Say de="Bis Samstag!" />, <Say de="Bis Montag!" />. The two
questions put the verb first; lesson 13 shows how that works.

## The insight

<Insight title="halb drei: halfway to three">

At 2:30, a German says <Say de="halb drei" />. Word for word that's "half three", but it
means **half of the way to three**. For the half hour, German looks ahead to the hour
that's coming; English looks back to the one just gone ("half past two"). Quarters work
as in English.

That makes it the most famous trap for British readers, because British English *also*
says "half two", and means 2:30 by it. Hear *halb drei* as "half three" and you turn up at
3:30, an hour late. Americans, who say "two-thirty", just learn it fresh. Dutch
(*half drie*) and Swedish (*halv tre*) count the German way; English is the odd one out
in its family.

| | |
|---|---|
| 2:00 | <Say de="zwei Uhr" /> |
| 2:15 | <Say de="Viertel nach zwei" /> (quarter past) |
| 2:30 | <Say de="halb drei" /> (half to three) |
| 2:45 | <Say de="Viertel vor drei" /> (quarter to) |
| 2:50 | <Say de="zehn vor drei" /> |

That's the clock people *speak*. Timetables, opening hours and appointments use the 24-hour
clock, read out exactly as written: 14:30 is <Say de="vierzehn Uhr dreißig" />. Ana used
both to be sure, and so should you, the first few times.

**One more thing the scene showed.** Ana said <Say de="Am Donnerstag arbeite ich." />, not
"Am Donnerstag ich arbeite". In a German statement **the verb is always second**. Usually
the subject comes first: <Say de="Ich arbeite am Dienstag." /> Put a time first instead,
and the subject steps behind the verb, so the verb keeps its place:

- <Say de="Ich habe um zwölf Zeit." /> → <Say de="Um zwölf habe ich Zeit." />
- <Say de="Ich arbeite am Dienstag." /> → <Say de="Am Dienstag arbeite ich." />

"Second" means the second building block, not the second word: *am Mittwoch um zehn* is
one block, like *am Donnerstag um zehn* in the scene. And *und* and *aber* don't count: <Say de="Aber um zwölf habe ich Zeit." />
English keeps scraps of this ("Here comes the bus"); German does it every time.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="viertel drei: the regional clock">

In parts of eastern and southern Germany and in Austria, many people say
<span lang="de">*viertel drei*</span> for 2:15 (a quarter of the way to three) and
<span lang="de">*dreiviertel drei*</span> for 2:45. It's the same look-ahead idea taken
further. *Viertel nach* and *Viertel vor* are understood everywhere, so use those, but
don't be thrown if you hear the other.

</Deeper>

<Deeper title="Sun, moon and a mid-week">

You know <Say de="die Sonne" /> and <Say de="der Mond" /> from lesson 5. <Say de="Sonntag" />
is the sun's day and <Say de="Montag" /> the moon's, just like Sunday and Monday.
<Say de="Donnerstag" /> is the day of Donar, the thunder god English calls Thor: Thursday.
<Say de="Mittwoch" /> simply means "mid-week", where English kept Woden's day, Wednesday.
In the north and east you'll also hear <span lang="de">*Sonnabend*</span> ("Sunday eve")
for <Say de="Samstag" />.

</Deeper>

## Sound: sp and st

At the start of a word, *sp* and *st* sound like "shp" and "sht": <Say de="spät" />,
<Say de="Stunde" />, <Say de="der Sport" />. Elsewhere they sound as in English: <Say de="das Fenster" />, <Say de="ist" />.

## How people really say it

**Be on time.** For an appointment, five minutes early is on time. If you'll be even
<span lang="de">*fünf Minuten*</span> late, send a message.

**A Termin for everything.** Germans book a <Say de="Termin" /> for the hairdresser, the
doctor and the city office (the *Bürgeramt*), where the next free slot can be weeks away.

**Auf Wiederhören.** On the phone, <Say de="Auf Wiedersehen!" /> ("until we see again")
becomes <Say de="Auf Wiederhören!" />, "until we hear again". Friends just say
<Say de="Tschüss!" />.

**Opening hours** are written in the 24-hour clock: *Mo–Fr 9–18 Uhr*. Most businesses
close on Sundays.

## Watch out

**halb drei is 2:30, not 3:30.** British "half three" is 3:30, which in German is
<Say de="halb vier" />.

**am for days, um for clock times.** <Say de="am Montag" />, <Say de="um drei Uhr" />.

**After a time word, the verb still comes second.** <Say de="Am Montag arbeite ich." />,
never "Am Montag ich arbeite".

**Uhr or Stunde?** <Say de="die Uhr" /> is the clock and the "o'clock" in
<Say de="drei Uhr" />; <Say de="die Stunde" /> is an hour of time:
<Say de="drei Stunden" />. And *arbeiten* adds an *e*, like *kostet* in lesson 4:
<Say de="du arbeitest" />, <Say de="er arbeitet" />.
