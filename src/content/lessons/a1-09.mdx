<Initial>

Eating out is where your German gets tested in real time: a menu, a waiter, a bill. This
lesson takes you through dinner in a Munich inn, from asking for a seat to tipping the way
Germans do. And it finally explains why lesson 2 said *du sprichst*, not *du sprechst*.

</Initial>

## The scene

<Dialogue title="A table, please" setting="A Wirtshaus in Munich, seven in the evening. Mei is from Singapore and new in Munich; Lukas is her colleague.">
  <Line who="Lukas" de="Entschuldigung, ist hier noch frei?" en="Excuse me, is this table free?" />
  <Line who="Herr Huber" de="Grüß Gott! Ja, bitte. Hier ist die Karte." en="Good evening! Yes, please. Here's the menu." />
  <Line who="Mei" de="Ich habe Hunger!" en="I'm hungry!" />
  <Line who="Lukas" de="Ich auch. Und Durst! Was nimmst du?" en="Me too. And thirsty! What are you having?" />
  <Line who="Mei" de="Hm. Ich esse kein Fleisch und keinen Fisch." en="Hmm. I don't eat meat or fish." />
  <Line who="Lukas" de="Dann die Knödel! Sie sind vegetarisch und sehr lecker. Ich nehme das Schnitzel." en="Then the dumplings! They're vegetarian and really good. I'm having the schnitzel." />
  <Line who="Herr Huber" de="Was bekommen Sie?" en="What can I get you?" />
  <Line who="Mei" de="Er nimmt das Schnitzel und ein Bier." en="He's having the schnitzel and a beer." />
  <Line who="Herr Huber" de="Groß oder klein?" en="Large or small?" />
  <Line who="Lukas" de="Groß, bitte!" en="Large, please!" />
  <Line who="Mei" de="Und ich möchte die Knödel und einen Salat. Und ein Wasser, bitte." en="And I'd like the dumplings and a salad. And a water, please." />
  <Line who="Herr Huber" de="Gern." en="Certainly." />
</Dialogue>

  <Line who="Lukas" de="Zahlen, bitte!" en="Could we pay, please?" />
  <Line who="Herr Huber" de="Zusammen oder getrennt?" en="Together or separately?" />
  <Line who="Mei" de="Getrennt, bitte." en="Separately, please." />
  <Line who="Herr Huber" de="Das Schnitzel und das Bier: einundzwanzig Euro vierzig." en="The schnitzel and the beer: twenty-one euros forty." />
  <Line who="Lukas" de="Dreiundzwanzig, bitte." en="Make it twenty-three, please." />
  <Line who="Herr Huber" de="Danke schön! Und die Knödel, der Salat und das Wasser: siebzehn Euro sechzig." en="Thank you! And the dumplings, the salad and the water: seventeen euros sixty." />
  <Line who="Mei" de="Neunzehn, bitte." en="Nineteen, please." />
  <Line who="Herr Huber" de="Danke schön! Auf Wiedersehen!" en="Thank you! Goodbye!" />

Listen to one verb change shape: Lukas says <Say de="ich nehme" />, asks
<Say de="Was nimmst du?" />, and Mei orders for him: <Say de="Er nimmt das Schnitzel." />
*nehme*, *nimmst*, *nimmt*. And notice how they tip: nobody leaves coins on the table.
They say the total they want to pay.

## Phrases to keep

<Chunk de="Ist hier noch frei?" literal="Is here still free?" means="Is this seat (table) free?" />

<Chunk de="Guten Appetit!" literal="Good appetite!" means="Enjoy your meal!" />

<Chunk de="Zahlen, bitte!" literal="Pay, please!" means="Could we have the bill, please?" />

<Chunk de="Zusammen oder getrennt?" literal="Together or separated?" means="One bill or separate?" />

<Chunk de="Stimmt so." literal="(It) is-right so." means="Keep the change." />

*Ist hier noch frei?* puts the verb first, like every yes-or-no question. Lesson 13 shows
how to build them yourself.

## The insight

<Insight title="The lost i in du nimmst">

You have met this pattern four times without its name: <Say de="du sprichst" /> in lesson
2, <Say de="sie spricht" /> in lesson 5, <Say de="er liest" /> in lesson 6, and tonight's
<Say de="du nimmst" />. Here it is whole.

Some verbs change their vowel, but **only for *du* and *er · sie · es***:

| | du | er · sie · es |
|---|---|---|
| <span lang="de">*nehmen*</span> | <Say de="du nimmst" /> | <Say de="er nimmt" /> |
| <span lang="de">*essen*</span> | <Say de="du isst" /> | <Say de="er isst" /> |
| <span lang="de">*sprechen*</span> | <Say de="du sprichst" /> | <Say de="er spricht" /> |
| <span lang="de">*lesen*</span> | <Say de="du liest" /> | <Say de="er liest" /> |
| <span lang="de">*fahren*</span> | <Say de="du fährst" /> | <Say de="er fährt" /> |

The endings are the ones you know. Only the vowel moves, nearly always in one of three ways:
*e* becomes *i*, *e* becomes *ie*, *a* becomes *ä*. In these verbs, *ich*, *wir*, *ihr*
and *sie* (they) keep the vowel of the infinitive, always: <Say de="ich nehme" />, <Say de="wir nehmen" />.

**Why just those two?** Go back a couple of thousand years, to the language German and
English both grew out of. Then, the *du* and *er* endings had an *i* sound in them. Say
*i* and your tongue moves up and forward; over generations, the vowel before it moved to
meet it. The *i* later wore away, but it left its mark on the vowel. The plural forms
never had that *i*, so their vowels stayed where they were. (*ich* had a similar ending
for a while, and medieval German said *ich nime*; then it fell back in line with *wir*.)

English has the same marks, in a different place. **foot → feet, mouse → mice, goose →
geese**: those plurals once ended in an *i* sound too, which pulled the vowel in a similar
way and then disappeared. *du nimmst* and *feet* are scars of the same old habit: a vowel
leaning towards an *i* that came after it.

So you don't need to memorise chaos. A verb either changes or it doesn't, and if it
does, only *du* and *er · sie · es* change. The word list shows those forms for every
verb, so you'll always know.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="Three ways to order">

All three work, and all three take the accusative from lesson 8, so *der* words become
*den* or *einen*:

- <Say de="Ich nehme den Salat." />: "I'll take", the most common.
- <Say de="Ich möchte einen Salat." />: "I'd like", a touch more polite.
- <Say de="Ich hätte gern einen Salat." />: lesson 4's bakery phrase, fine everywhere.

Waiters often ask <Say de="Was bekommen Sie?" /> ("what do you get?"), and you can answer
<Say de="Ich bekomme einen Salat." /> too.

*möchten* has one odd habit: no *-t* for *er*. <Say de="ich möchte" />,
<Say de="du möchtest" />, <Say de="er möchte" />. Lesson 12 shows why it behaves like that.

</Deeper>

<Deeper title="nehmen in English">

English once had this verb too: Old English *niman*, to take. It survives in hiding.
**numb** first meant "taken", seized by cold or shock, and **nimble** first meant quick to
take. *Nim*, meaning to pinch or steal, hung on in English slang for centuries.

</Deeper>

## Sound: z and j

**z is always "ts"**, even at the start of a word: <Say de="zahlen" />,
<Say de="zusammen" />, <Say de="zwanzig" />. The same goes for *tz*: <Say de="jetzt" />.
An English *z* as in *zoo* sounds like an *s* to German ears, so make the *t* in "ts"
clear.

**j is English y:** <Say de="ja" />, <Say de="jetzt" />.

## How people really say it

**Seat yourself.** In a Wirtshaus or café you usually find your own table.
<Say de="Ist hier noch frei?" /> asks whether a table or seat is free: ask
<Say de="der Kellner" /> (the waiter), or the people already sitting there, because sharing
a long table is normal. A table with a *Stammtisch*
sign is kept for regulars: don't sit there.

**die Karte is the menu.** In lesson 4 it was the card you pay with; in a restaurant it is
also the menu. Same word, both jobs.

**Water costs money.** *Wasser* on a menu means bottled water, still or sparkling, and tap
water isn't brought automatically. Some places will bring it if you ask; some won't.

**Not every Knödel is vegetarian.** Lukas means the bread dumplings on this menu, but
*Leberknödel* (liver) and *Speckknödel* (bacon) are just as Bavarian. Ask before you order.

**Beer sizes.** In Munich you'll also hear *eine Halbe* (half a litre) and, in beer gardens,
*eine Maß*: a whole litre.

**Paying.** Call <Say de="Zahlen, bitte!" /> or ask for <Say de="die Rechnung" />. The
waiter asks <Say de="Zusammen oder getrennt?" />, and paying separately is completely
normal, among friends and colleagues alike.

**The tip (<Say de="das Trinkgeld" />) is spoken, not left.** Around 5 to 10 percent is usual. Say the total you want to
pay as you hand over the money (the bill is 18.40, you say <Say de="Zwanzig, bitte." />),
or <Say de="Stimmt so." /> if the change is the tip.

**Prost!** Before the first sip, glasses touch and everyone says <Say de="Prost!" />,
looking each other in the eye.

## Watch out

**Only du and er · sie · es change.** <Say de="wir nehmen" />, <Say de="ihr nehmt" />,
<Say de="sie nehmen" />: no change.

**nehmen drops its h and doubles its m too.** <Say de="du nimmst" />,
<Say de="er nimmt" />.

**er isst and er ist sound the same.** One means "he eats", the other "he is". Context
tells them apart, and *du isst* is never "du esst".

**bekommen is not "become".** <Say de="Ich bekomme einen Salat." /> means "I'll have a
salad", not that you are turning into one.
