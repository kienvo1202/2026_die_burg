<Initial>

Saturday morning, a shared fridge, and the moment every flatshare knows: someone opens
the door and there is nothing in it. This lesson gives you the words for what you have
and what you don't, and finally keeps a promise from the bakery in lesson 4: why
*ein Kaffee* sometimes becomes *einen Kaffee*.

</Initial>

## The insight

<Insight title="den is the him of the article">

English still changes some words when they stop being the one *doing* something and
become the one something is *done to*: **he** sees, but you see **him**. **She** →
**her**, **we** → **us**, **they** → **them**, **who** → **whom**. Grammar calls this the
*accusative*: the form for the thing you have, want, need, buy or see.

German does the same, in one more place: the article of *der* words.

| | the one doing | the thing had, wanted, needed |
|---|---|---|
| <span lang="de">*der*</span> words | <Say de="der Käse" />, <Say de="ein Käse" />, <Say de="kein Käse" /> | <Say de="den Käse" />, <Say de="einen Käse" />, <Say de="keinen Käse" /> |
| <span lang="de">*die*</span> words | <Say de="die Milch" />, <Say de="eine Milch" /> | **the same** |
| <span lang="de">*das*</span> words | <Say de="das Ei" />, <Say de="ein Ei" /> | **the same** |
| plurals | <Say de="die Eier" /> | **the same** |

That's all: **only *der* words change**, and they always end in *-n*: *den*, *einen*,
*keinen*. Everything else stays exactly as you learned it.

Compare:

- <Say de="Der Käse ist teuer." />: the cheese is doing the being.
- <Say de="Ich kaufe den Käse." />: the cheese is what I buy.

Old English did this with its articles too. Remember lesson 4's *se*, *sēo*, *þæt*?
The *se* of a thing you had or wanted became *þone*, the twin of German *den*. English
lost it from its articles, but still has the idea in *him*, *her*, *us* and *them*.

**So here is lesson 4's promise kept:** you order <Say de="einen Kaffee" />, because
*der Kaffee* is a *der* word and it is the thing you want. But
<Say de="eine Brezel" /> and <Say de="ein Brötchen" /> don't change here.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="kein or nicht?">

Both mean *not*, but they do different jobs.

- <Say de="kein" /> goes in front of a noun, where *ein* or no article would be:
  <Say de="Wir haben keine Milch." /> (not "nicht Milch"). It takes the same endings as
  *ein*: <span lang="de">*kein Brot*</span>, <span lang="de">*keine Milch*</span>,
  <span lang="de">*keinen Käse*</span>, and <span lang="de">*keine Eier*</span> for plurals.
- <Say de="nicht" /> does everything else: verbs, adjectives, *gern*:
  <Say de="Der Käse ist nicht teuer." /> <Say de="Ich trinke nicht gern Tee." />

Think of *kein* as *nicht + ein* melted into one word.

</Deeper>

## Sound: long and short vowels

German vowels come long or short, and the spelling tells you which.

- **A double consonant after a vowel makes it short:** <Say de="Butter" />,
  <Say de="Wasser" />.
- **h after a vowel, or a doubled vowel, makes it long:** <Say de="Kühlschrank" />'s *ü*,
  <Say de="Tee" />, <Say de="leer" />.

Listen to the difference: <Say de="Milch" /> (short *i*) and <Say de="Käse" /> (long *ä*).
Getting the length right matters a lot for being understood.

## How people really say it

**Sunday is closed.** Supermarkets in Germany and Austria close on Sundays, so Saturday is
the big shopping day, and the queues show it. In Germany, bakeries (for a few morning
hours), petrol stations and shops in railway stations are the exceptions; in Cologne, so
are the corner kiosks, the *Büdchen*.

**Bring a bag.** Light plastic bags are banned in Germany and Austria; shops sell paper or
reusable ones, so most people bring their own.

**Pfand.** Most bottles and cans carry a deposit, usually 25 cents for a single-use
plastic bottle. You get it back from a machine in the shop. Don't throw them away; someone
will collect them if you leave them beside a bin.

**der or das Joghurt?** In Germany it is mostly *der Joghurt*; in Austria it is
<span lang="de">*das Joghurt*</span>. Julia from Graz was right too: the article can
change with the country.

**Oat milk** is everywhere in German cities.

## Watch out

**Only der words change.** <Say de="einen Käse" />, but <Say de="eine Milch" />,
<Say de="ein Ei" />, <Say de="zwei Eier" />.

**kein, not nicht, before a noun.** <Say de="Ich habe keine Milch." />, not "Ich habe nicht
Milch".

**Food in general often has no article.** <Say de="Wir brauchen Milch und Käse." /> is
completely normal for a shopping list. *Einen Käse* means one cheese: a single piece or pack.

**Not "Ich bin hungrig".** It is understood, but Germans usually say
<span lang="de">*Ich habe Hunger*</span>: I have hunger.
