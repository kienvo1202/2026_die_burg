<Initial>

Plans are where you say what you *can* do, what you *want* to do and what you *must* do
instead. German has the same three verbs as English, nearly the same words, and one trap
that makes English speakers sound oddly determined. This lesson plans a weekend in the
mountains above Innsbruck.

</Initial>

## The scene

<Dialogue title="Saturday?" setting="A group chat, Thursday evening. Sophie is from Innsbruck; Mateo is from Buenos Aires and new in town; Lisa is Sophie's friend.">
  <Line who="Sophie" de="Am Samstag will ich wandern. Kommst du mit, Mateo?" en="On Saturday I want to go hiking. Are you coming, Mateo?" />
  <Line who="Mateo" de="Leider nicht. Am Samstag muss ich arbeiten." en="Unfortunately not. On Saturday I have to work." />
  <Line who="Sophie" de="Schade! Und du, Lisa? Was willst du machen?" en="What a shame! And you, Lisa? What do you want to do?" />
  <Line who="Lisa" de="Ich kann nicht weit laufen. Aber ich will schwimmen." en="I can't walk far. But I want to swim." />
  <Line who="Mateo" de="Schwimmen? Der See ist kalt, oder? Und ich kann nicht gut schwimmen …" en="Swimming? The lake's cold, isn't it? And I can't swim very well…" />
  <Line who="Lisa" de="Kein Problem! Er ist warm." en="No problem! It's warm." />
  <Line who="Sophie" de="Wie wär's mit Sonntag? Am Morgen wandern wir, und am Nachmittag gehen wir alle schwimmen." en="How about Sunday? In the morning we hike, and in the afternoon we all go swimming." />
  <Line who="Lisa" de="Gut! Am Sonntag ist das Wetter schön." en="Good! On Sunday the weather's nice." />
  <Line who="Mateo" de="Vielleicht … ja, am Sonntag kann ich. Wann?" en="Maybe… yes, on Sunday I can. When?" />
  <Line who="Sophie" de="Um sieben! Wir müssen früh fahren." en="At seven! We have to go early." />
  <Line who="Mateo" de="Um sieben? Am Sonntag? Gut …" en="At seven? On a Sunday? All right…" />
</Dialogue>

  <Line who="Sophie" de="Was gibt es heute?" en="What is there today?" />

Listen to where the second verb goes: <Say de="Am Samstag muss ich arbeiten." /> In English
the two verbs stay together ("I have to work"). In German *muss* stands second and
*arbeiten* waits at the very end.

## Phrases to keep

<Chunk de="Kommst du mit?" literal="Come you along?" means="Are you coming (with us)?" />

<Chunk de="Leider nicht." literal="Regrettably not." means="Unfortunately not." />

<Chunk de="Schade!" literal="Pity!" means="What a shame!" />

<Chunk de="Wie wär's mit …?" literal="How would-it-be with …?" means="How about …?" />

*Leider nicht* is the polite no: it says you'd like to, but can't.

## The insight

<Insight title="er kann, not er kannt">

<Say de="können" />, <Say de="wollen" /> and <Say de="müssen" /> are English **can**,
**will** and **must**, and two habits matter. The first shows they're family.

**First: no ending for ich and er.** Almost every other verb you know adds *-t* for *er*: *er
wohnt*, *er nimmt*. These don't.

| | ich · er · sie · es | du | wir · sie |
|---|---|---|---|
| <span lang="de">*können*</span> | <Say de="kann" /> | <Say de="kannst" /> | <Say de="können" /> |
| <span lang="de">*wollen*</span> | <Say de="will" /> | <Say de="willst" /> | <Say de="wollen" /> |
| <span lang="de">*müssen*</span> | <Say de="muss" /> | <Say de="musst" /> | <Say de="müssen" /> |

English does exactly the same: **he can**, never "he cans"; **he must**, never "he
musts". The reason is old. Most of these verbs began as past tenses, put to work as
presents, and in the past tense "I" and "he" share one form with no ending. English still shows it:
*I sang, he sang*. (*wollen* came by a different road but joined the pattern.) That's
also why lesson 9's <Say de="er möchte" /> has no *-t*: *möchte* is built on a past form too, like English *might*.

**Second: the other verb goes to the end.** In English the two verbs sit together: "I
can cook well". In German the modal stands second, as the verb does in every statement (lesson 10), and
the other verb waits at the very end, unchanged:

- <Say de="Ich kann gut kochen." />
- <Say de="Am Sonntag müssen wir früh fahren." />
- <Say de="Ich möchte schwimmen." />

The two verbs make a frame round the rest of the sentence. German uses the same frame
again for the spoken past and for verbs like the one in <Say de="Kommst du mit?" />, so it's worth getting used to now. It
also explains lesson 8's chunk <Say de="Kannst du … kaufen?" />: *kannst* near the
front, *kaufen* at the end.

**The trap: ich will means "I want".** <Say de="Ich will schwimmen." /> is "I want to
swim", not "I will swim". Old English *willan* meant "to want" too; English slowly turned
it into the future, and German never did.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="müssen nicht: you don't have to">

<Say de="Ich muss nicht arbeiten." /> means "I don't have to work", a happy sentence.
It does **not** mean "I mustn't work". For "mustn't", German uses another modal,
*dürfen* (to be allowed), which comes in A1.2: *du darfst nicht*, you mustn't.

</Deeper>

<Deeper title="du läufst: a rarer change">

Lesson 9 said vowel-changing verbs nearly always change in one of three ways. Here is one of the
rarer ones: *au* becomes *äu*. <Say de="ich laufe" />, <Say de="du läufst" />,
<Say de="er läuft" />. Same rule as before: only *du* and *er · sie · es*.

</Deeper>

## Sound: where the stress falls

German stresses the **first syllable** of most simple words: <Say de="wandern" /> (WAN-dern),
<Say de="schwimmen" /> (SCHWIM-men), <Say de="arbeiten" /> (AR-bei-ten),
<Say de="laufen" /> (LAU-fen).

Some words stress a later syllable, often ones borrowed from French or Latin: *Problem*
(pro-BLEM), and <Say de="natürlich" /> (na-TÜR-lich), built on *Natur*. And *Kaffee*
splits by region: KAF-feh in most of Germany, kaf-FEH in Austria. Small prefixes such as
*be-* and *ver-* are never stressed; you'll meet them with their verbs.

## How people really say it

**Everyone says hello on the mountain (<Say de="der Berg" />).** In the Alps, hikers greet everyone they pass:
<Say de="Servus!" />, <Say de="Grüß Gott!" />, or in Tyrol *Griaß di!* And on the mountain
people often use *du*, even with strangers. That's why Frau Gruber says
<Say de="Was wollt ihr essen?" />, with *ihr*, to three people she has never met.

**Huts.** A <Say de="Hütte" /> in the mountains, often owned by an Alpine club, serves simple
hot food and lets hikers stay the night. Bring cash.

**Plan ahead, a little.** Weekend plans are usually made a few days before. Among friends
a spontaneous <Say de="Kommst du mit?" /> is fine.

**Innsbruck** has mountains at the edge of town: a funicular and cable cars (the
*Nordkettenbahnen*) go up from near the centre.

## Watch out

**ich will = I want, not I will.** <Say de="Ich will Kaffee." /> is "I want coffee".

**muss nicht = don't have to.** <Say de="Ich muss nicht arbeiten." />: no work today.

**No -t for ich and er.** <Say de="er kann" />, <Say de="er will" />, <Say de="er muss" />,
never "er kannt".

**der See or die See?** <Say de="der See" /> is a lake; *die See* is the sea. Same word,
different article, different water.
