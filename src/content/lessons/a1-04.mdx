<Initial>

Every German day starts at the bakery. Before work, before school, before anyone has said
a word to you, someone behind a counter will look up and wait for your order. This lesson
gets you through that moment, and through the thing that makes German nouns look so
hard: der, die and das. They turn out to be something English had too.

</Initial>

choices. That is today's idea.

## The insight

<Insight title="English had der, die and das too">

Every German noun belongs to one of three groups, and the word for *the* tells you which:

| <span lang="de">*der*</span> words | <span lang="de">*die*</span> words | <span lang="de">*das*</span> words |
|---|---|---|
| <Say de="der Kuchen" /> | <Say de="die Brezel" /> | <Say de="das Brötchen" /> |
| <Say de="der Kaffee" /> | <Say de="die Bäckerei" /> | <Say de="das Brot" /> |

Grammar books call these masculine, feminine and neuter (*gender*), but the group belongs
to the **word**, not to the thing. A cake is not male. And <Say de="das Mädchen" />, the
girl, is a *das* word.

That sounds like a German eccentricity until you look at Old English. A thousand years
ago, English had three basic words for *the* as well: *se* for masculine words, *sēo* for
feminine ones and *þæt* for neuter ones (*þ* is an old letter for *th*). Over the next few
centuries English merged them into a single *the*. One of them survived on its own:
*þæt* became **that**.

So when you ask <Say de="Was kostet das?" />, you are saying "What costs *that*?" with
the very same word. English didn't avoid der, die and das. It had them and wore them
down.

**What this means for you:** a German noun without its article is only half a word.
Learn <Say de="die Brezel" />, never just *Brezel*. In the word lists the article comes
in colour: blue for *der*, red for *die*, green for *das*. Picture the colour, say the
article, and it sticks.

*A* follows the same groups, with one shape for two of them:

| | *the* | *a* |
|---|---|---|
| <span lang="de">*der*</span> words | <Say de="der Kuchen" /> | <Say de="ein Kuchen" /> |
| <span lang="de">*die*</span> words | <Say de="die Brezel" /> | <Say de="eine Brezel" /> |
| <span lang="de">*das*</span> words | <Say de="das Stück" /> | <Say de="ein Stück" /> |

And one more thing you may have noticed: every German noun starts with a capital letter,
wherever it is in the sentence. <span lang="de">*Brot*</span>,
<span lang="de">*Kaffee*</span>, <span lang="de">*Mädchen*</span>. It makes nouns easy to
spot on a page.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="Why Brötchen and Mädchen are das">

The ending *-chen* means "little": <span lang="de">*Brötchen*</span> is a little bread,
<span lang="de">*Mädchen*</span> a little maid. And every noun made small with *-chen* is a
*das* word, whatever it names: this rule has no exceptions. (Careful: <span lang="de">*der Kuchen*</span>
only happens to end in *-chen*; it isn't a little *Ku*.)

English has a distant cousin of this ending in *-kin*: a *napkin* was once a little cloth,
a *lambkin* a little lamb. English kept the ending in a few words; German uses it every
day.

</Deeper>

## Sound: ö and ü

<span lang="de">*Brötchen*</span>, <span lang="de">*fünf*</span>,
<span lang="de">*Grüß Gott*</span>: two sounds English doesn't have. The trick is in the
lips.

- **ü**: say "ee", then round your lips as if to whistle, without moving your tongue:
  <Say de="Grüß Gott!" />. In <Say de="fünf" /> it is the same sound, just shorter.
- **ö**: say "ay" (without the English glide), then round your lips the same way: <Say de="Brötchen" />,
  <Say de="Österreich" />.

If it feels like your mouth is saying two things at once, you are doing it right.

## How people really say it

**Bitte schön?** Behind a counter it means "What can I get you?". Handing something over
it means "Here you are", and after a thank-you it means "You're welcome".
<span lang="de">*Bitte*</span> does almost every polite job there is.

**The roll has many names.** <Say de="das Brötchen" /> is understood everywhere, but in
Berlin you will hear *Schrippe*, in Bavaria and Austria *Semmel*, in the south-west
*Weckle* or *Weck*, and Switzerland has its own names again. Order with the local word and you will
get a smile.

**Bread is serious.** Germany records thousands of bread varieties, and many bakeries
open by six. Some open on Sunday mornings too, which is why the bakery queue is part of a
German weekend.

**Cash or card?** Cards are widely accepted now, but small bakeries may still prefer cash,
or have a minimum for cards. A few coins in your pocket never hurt.

## Watch out

**Learn the article with the noun.** <span lang="de">*die* Brezel</span>,
<span lang="de">*das* Brötchen</span>: guessing from the thing, or from English, will
fail you.

**Order with Ich hätte gern.** "Ich will…" (I want) sounds blunt at a counter.

**Ordering a der word changes ein.** You will hear
<span lang="de">*Ich hätte gern einen Kaffee*</span>: when you order something, *ein*
becomes *einen* for *der* words (only for them). A later lesson shows why. Until then,
order *die* and *das* words, or a number: <Say de="zwei Kaffee, bitte" />.

**Prices:** 2,40 € is said <Say de="zwei Euro vierzig" />. Germans write a comma where
English writes a point, and say *Euro*, not *Euros*, after a number.

**Two coffees is usually zwei Kaffee.** When you order, *Kaffee* normally stays singular
after a number: <Say de="zwei Kaffee, bitte" />.
