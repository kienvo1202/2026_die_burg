<Initial>

Tom has twenty minutes to decide whether he wants to live in a stranger's flat, and they
have twenty minutes to decide whether they want him. Welcome to the WG-Casting. This
lesson gives you the words for a room and what it is like, and one small word that
German uses in a way English gave up centuries ago.

</Initial>

Look at what Lena called the kitchen: <Say de="sie" />, "she". The bathroom was
<Say de="es" />, and the old wardrobe <Say de="er" />, "he". Nobody in that flat thinks a
wardrobe is a man. That is today's idea.

## The insight

<Insight title="The wardrobe is a he">

In lesson 4 you met *der*, *die* and *das*, and learned that the group belongs to the word,
not the thing. Here is where it pays off. When German says "it", it picks the pronoun that
matches the article:

| the word | "it" | |
|---|---|---|
| <Say de="der Schrank" /> | <Say de="er" /> | "he" |
| <Say de="die Küche" /> | <Say de="sie" /> | "she" |
| <Say de="das Bad" /> | <Say de="es" /> | "it" |

So <Say de="Der Schrank ist alt, aber er ist groß." /> is, word for word, "the wardrobe is
old, but *he* is big". To a German ear that is simply correct.

English did exactly this a thousand years ago. In Old English the sun was a feminine word,
*sēo sunne*, so the sun was "she"; the moon was masculine, *se mōna*, so the moon was
"he". German never stopped:

- <Say de="die Sonne" />: <span lang="de">*sie*</span>, she
- <Say de="der Mond" />: <span lang="de">*er*</span>, he

The good news: if you learned the article with the noun, you already know the pronoun.
*der* gives *er*, *die* gives *sie*, *das* gives *es*. For people, use *er* or *sie* by who they are:
<Say de="Wie ist Lena? Sie kommt aus Freiburg." />

**The verb after er, sie and es** almost always ends in *-t*: <Say de="sie kommt" />,
<Say de="er wohnt" />, <Say de="es kostet" />. And <span lang="de">*sein*</span> becomes
<Say de="ist" />:

| | <span lang="de">*wohnen*</span> | <span lang="de">*sein*</span> |
|---|---|---|
| **ich** | <Say de="ich wohne" /> | <Say de="ich bin" /> |
| **du** | <Say de="du wohnst" /> | <Say de="du bist" /> |
| **er · sie · es** | <Say de="er wohnt" /> | <Say de="sie ist" /> |
| **Sie** | <Say de="Sie wohnen" /> | <Say de="Sie sind" /> |

**And the adjective after ist never changes.** <Say de="Das Zimmer ist klein." />
<Say de="Die Küche ist klein." /> <Say de="Der Schrank ist klein." /> Same *klein* every
time, whatever the gender.

</Insight>

<Deeper title="Sie spricht: the vowel change again">

Tom said <Say de="sie spricht" />, not *sie sprecht*. Remember <Say de="du sprichst" />
from lesson 2? The verbs that change their vowel for *du* change it for *er*, *sie* and
*es* too. Same few dozen verbs, same two forms. A later lesson shows the pattern behind
them.

</Deeper>

## Sound: hard endings

At the end of a word, German hardens three letters: **b** sounds like *p*, **d** like *t*,
**g** like *k*.

- <Say de="das Bad" /> sounds like "baht"
- <Say de="Freiburg" /> sounds like "FRY-boork"
- <Say de="und" /> sounds like "oont"

Add an ending and the soft sound comes back: one <span lang="de">*Bad*</span> ("baht"),
two <span lang="de">*Bäder*</span> ("BAY-der"). You heard it in lesson 1 already:
<Say de="Guten Tag!" />, "tahk".

One exception: **-ig** at the end of a word sounds like *-ich* in standard German, so
<Say de="günstig" /> is "GÜN-stich". In the south you will also hear "-ik".

## How people really say it

**The WG.** In a <Say de="WG" /> (say "vay-gay") each person has a room and everyone
shares the kitchen and bathroom. It is a very common way to live for students and
people in their twenties. When a room is free, the flatmates invite candidates and choose
one: the WG-Casting. Expect <span lang="de">*du*</span> from the first second.

**Warm or cold?** A rent is quoted <span lang="de">*kalt*</span> (cold: the flat only) or
<span lang="de">*warm*</span> (with heating and service charges). Lena said
<span lang="de">*warm*</span>: always ask which.

**No kitchen?** Unfurnished flats in Germany often come without a kitchen. Tenants buy
one, and take it with them when they move, or sell it to the next tenant.

**Open the windows.** Several times a day, even in winter, Germans open the windows wide
for a few minutes, then close them. It is called *Stoßlüften*, and flatmates notice if
you don't.

## Watch out

**The pronoun follows the article.** <span lang="de">*Der Tisch*</span> is
<Say de="er" />, never *es*. Only *das* words take <span lang="de">*es*</span>.

**billig or günstig?** *Billig* means cheap, and can sound like poor quality.
<Say de="günstig" /> means a good price, and is what you say about a nice flat.

**gemütlich has no English twin.** Cosy, comfortable and welcoming at once: a room, a
café, an evening.

**Nothing changes after ist.** The adjective stays as it is:
<Say de="Die Wohnung ist schön." />
